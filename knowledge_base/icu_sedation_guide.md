# ICU Sedation and Analgesia

## Analgesia-First Principle

The analgesia-first (analgosedation) approach prioritises pain control before initiating sedatives, recognising that uncontrolled pain is the most common driver of agitation in mechanically ventilated patients. Addressing pain first reduces total sedative requirements, shortens mechanical ventilation duration, and improves short and long-term outcomes. The 2018 Society of Critical Care Medicine PADIS guidelines (Pain, Agitation/Sedation, Delirium, Immobility, Sleep) provide the current evidence-based framework and emphasise targeting light sedation (RASS –1 to 0) as the default for all mechanically ventilated patients unless a specific indication for deeper sedation exists.

The ABCDEF bundle (Assess, prevent and manage pain; Both SATs and SBTs; Choice of analgesia and sedation; Delirium assessment; Early mobility; Family engagement) operationalises the PADIS guidelines in clinical practice. Systematic bundle implementation is associated with significant reductions in 90-day mortality, delirium, mechanical ventilation duration, and ICU and hospital length of stay (ICU Liberation Collaborative, 2018).

## Pain Assessment Tools

Pain must be assessed at least every 4 hours and before and after procedures. For patients who can communicate, the Numeric Rating Scale (NRS, 0–10) is used. For non-verbal or sedated patients: the Behavioural Pain Scale (BPS, range 3–12; target ≤5) assesses facial expression, upper limb movement, and compliance with ventilation; the Critical-Care Pain Observation Tool (CPOT, range 0–8; target ≤2) assesses facial expression, body movements, muscle tension, and ventilator compliance. Both have equivalent validity; institutional standardisation on one tool improves inter-rater reliability.

## Analgesic Agents

### Opioids

Intravenous opioids remain the cornerstone of analgesia for moderate-to-severe pain in the ICU. Fentanyl is preferred for acute severe pain and procedural analgesia: rapid onset (1–2 minutes), short duration (30–60 minutes), and easy titratability make it ideal for bolus dosing. Fentanyl is safe in renal failure (no active metabolite accumulation) and haemodynamically stable patients. Remifentanil infusion (0.05–0.2 mcg/kg/min) provides ultra-short-acting analgesia with context-insensitive half-life regardless of infusion duration — useful in patients requiring frequent neurological assessment (e.g., post-neurosurgery), enabling rapid offset without prolonged sedation.

Morphine is avoided in significant renal impairment (eGFR <30): the active metabolite morphine-6-glucuronide (M6G) accumulates and causes prolonged sedation and respiratory depression. Hydromorphone (2–4 mg SC/IV every 4 hours PRN) is a potent alternative (5× more potent than morphine) with shorter duration and fewer histamine-releasing effects, suitable in renal impairment at reduced doses. All opioids carry risk of ileus, urinary retention, and respiratory depression; dose titration guided by pain scores and clinical status is essential.

### Non-Opioid Adjuncts

Non-opioid analgesic adjuncts reduce opioid consumption (opioid-sparing strategy) and should be used in all ICU patients unless contraindicated. Regular paracetamol (acetaminophen) 1 g IV or PO every 6 hours reduces opioid requirements by 20–30% in postoperative ICU patients and is the safest analgesic adjunct; avoid only in severe hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh C).

Low-dose ketamine infusion (0.1–0.5 mg/kg/hour) provides analgesia through NMDA receptor antagonism without respiratory depression. It is particularly valuable in opioid-tolerant patients (trauma, burns, substance use disorder), where standard opioid doses may be insufficient. Ketamine also preserves airway reflexes and bronchodilates — it can be used as a bolus (1–2 mg/kg IV) for procedural analgesia or RSI. At sub-anaesthetic doses, psychomimetic side effects are uncommon but can be attenuated with concurrent low-dose midazolam or dexmedetomidine.

Intravenous ketorolac (15–30 mg every 6 hours, maximum 5 days) and other NSAIDs are effective for somatic pain but should be avoided in renal impairment (eGFR <60 as a relative caution, <30 as contraindication), coagulopathy, thrombocytopenia, or high GI bleeding risk. Selective COX-2 inhibitors (parecoxib 40 mg IV) may be preferable in patients with GI risk.

Gabapentin (300–900 mg PO twice daily) and pregabalin (75–150 mg PO twice daily) reduce neuropathic pain and opioid requirements in patients with chronic pain syndromes or spinal cord injury. Monitor for excess sedation and respiratory depression — dose reduction required in renal impairment. Lidocaine infusion (0.5–1.5 mg/kg/hour) has shown opioid-sparing effects in abdominal surgical patients and is used in some centres for refractory neuropathic pain.

## Sedation Targets

### RASS and Light Sedation Goals

The Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale (RASS) is the most widely validated sedation assessment tool. Scores range from –5 (unarousable) to +4 (combative). The target for most mechanically ventilated patients is RASS –1 to 0 (lightly sedated or alert/calm), assessed at least every 4 hours. Deep sedation (RASS –3 to –5) is independently associated with prolonged mechanical ventilation, increased ICU delirium, ICU-acquired weakness, post-ICU psychological disturbance, and 30-day and 180-day mortality.

Specific indications for deep sedation include: severe ARDS with neuromuscular blockade (RASS –4 to –5), refractory intracranial hypertension, therapeutic hypothermia post-cardiac arrest, and status epilepticus refractory to antiepileptic drugs. These indications should be explicitly documented, with daily reassessment of continued need. Spontaneous awakening trials (SATs) — interruption of sedation to allow assessment of neurological status and potential extubation — should be performed daily in patients without contraindications.

### Sedation Monitoring

Daily sedation assessment should combine the RASS with an objective measure of drug effect where feasible. Processed EEG monitoring (bispectral index, BIS; target 40–60 for adequate sedation, 60–80 for light sedation) reduces the risk of inadvertent deep sedation but does not replace clinical assessment. BIS is most valuable during neuromuscular blockade, when clinical sedation scoring is impossible.

Drug plasma levels are not routinely used for sedation titration but may be helpful in prolonged propofol or midazolam infusions to identify accumulation. Systematic daily documentation of sedation level, target, and rationale for any deviation from light sedation targets enables multidisciplinary review and reduces sedation drift.

## Sedative Agents

### Propofol

Propofol is the preferred IV sedative for most mechanically ventilated ICU patients due to rapid onset (30–60 seconds), short duration, and context-sensitive half-life that enables fast awakening even after prolonged infusions. Standard infusion rates: 0.3–4.0 mg/kg/hour for ICU sedation. It is also the agent of choice for procedural sedation (e.g., bronchoscopy) at bolus doses of 0.5–1.5 mg/kg IV.

Propofol infusion syndrome (PRIS) is a rare but potentially fatal complication characterised by metabolic acidosis, rhabdomyolysis, hyperkalaemia, lipaemia, and cardiac failure or arrhythmias. Risk factors include doses >4 mg/kg/hour for >48 hours, carbohydrate-restricted diets, catecholamine or glucocorticoid infusions, and mitochondrial disorders. Monitor triglycerides every 48 hours (PRIS risk if >4.5 mmol/L) and CK every 48–72 hours during prolonged infusions. The lipid content of propofol (1.1 kcal/mL for 1% formulation) should be factored into nutritional calculations. If PRIS is suspected, immediately discontinue propofol and switch sedative.

### Dexmedetomidine

Dexmedetomidine is a selective alpha-2 agonist that produces sedation, anxiolysis, and analgesia through central alpha-2 receptor activation in the locus coeruleus, without causing respiratory depression. Patients remain rousable, able to follow commands, and are more cooperative with physiotherapy and weaning — this "cooperative sedation" is its primary advantage over propofol and benzodiazepines.

Standard infusion: 0.2–1.4 mcg/kg/hour; no loading dose required for most patients (reduces haemodynamic side effects). Dexmedetomidine is preferred for sedation in patients at high delirium risk (MENDS2 trial), during ventilator weaning when agitation complicates extubation readiness, and in alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal. The PRODEX and MIDEX trials demonstrated non-inferiority to propofol and midazolam for sedation efficacy with shorter time to extubation. Main adverse effects: bradycardia (most common, usually not requiring treatment; avoid in pre-existing heart block or sick sinus syndrome) and hypotension. Rebound hypertension may occur on abrupt discontinuation.

### Benzodiazepines

Benzodiazepines (midazolam infusion, lorazepam bolus) are third-line sedative agents for mechanically ventilated patients. Prolonged midazolam infusions cause unpredictable sedation depth due to accumulation of the active metabolite 1-hydroxymidazolam in renal impairment, prolonging awakening and weaning. Multiple meta-analyses and trials (MIDEX, PRODEX) demonstrate longer mechanical ventilation duration and higher delirium rates with midazolam compared to propofol or dexmedetomidine.

Indications where benzodiazepines remain first-line: alcohol withdrawal seizures and delirium tremens (lorazepam 2–4 mg IV bolus, titrated by CIWA-Ar score); active convulsive status epilepticus (lorazepam 4 mg IV, repeat once); procedural anxiolysis where short-acting benzodiazepine is preferred (midazolam 1–2 mg IV). Critically ill patients who have been on prolonged benzodiazepine infusions are at high risk of withdrawal on discontinuation — taper by 10% per day with symptom monitoring.

## Delirium

### Screening

ICU delirium affects 20–80% of mechanically ventilated patients depending on population and definition, and is independently associated with prolonged ventilation, increased 6-month and 12-month mortality, and persistent cognitive impairment (post-ICU cognitive impairment, or PICS-cognitive). The Confusion Assessment Method for the ICU (CAM-ICU) is the most validated screening tool: positive when acute change or fluctuation in mental status is present AND inattention AND either altered level of consciousness or disorganised thinking. Assess every 8–12 hours using the standardised CAM-ICU flowsheet.

The Intensive Care Delirium Screening Checklist (ICDSC, range 0–8; score ≥4 = delirium) is an alternative with continuous observation rather than point-in-time assessment, potentially detecting subsyndromal delirium. Both tools have sensitivity 70–80% and specificity 90–95% for delirium detection when used correctly.

### Non-Pharmacological Prevention

Non-pharmacological delirium prevention is the primary evidence-based strategy and should be implemented for all ICU patients. Systematic review demonstrates that bundle-based multicomponent interventions reduce delirium prevalence by 30–50%.

Key components of the ABCDEF bundle applied to delirium prevention: early mobility (sitting, standing, walking as soon as haemodynamically stable); sleep promotion (minimising nocturnal interventions, reducing light and noise after 22:00, earplugs and eye masks for patients); reorientation (verbal reorientation at each interaction: patient name, date, location, reason for hospitalisation); cognitive stimulation (familiar objects, family photographs, portable music devices); correction of sensory deficits (hearing aids and glasses); and family engagement and presence.

Avoidance of delirium-promoting drugs: minimise benzodiazepines and anticholinergics; avoid H2 blockers if alternatives are available; prefer dexmedetomidine for sedation. Ensuring adequate analgesia (uncontrolled pain is a major delirium precipitant), maintaining sleep-wake cycles, promoting early nutrition, and targeting light sedation all contribute to delirium prevention.

### Pharmacological Treatment

Pharmacological treatment of established ICU delirium has limited high-quality evidence. Haloperidol (2.5–5 mg IV bolus every 6 hours; maximum 20 mg/day) does not reduce delirium duration or improve outcomes in unselected ICU patients (MIND-USA and HOPE-ICU trials), but may be used for rapid control of severe agitation threatening patient or staff safety. Avoid if QTc >500 ms or history of torsades de pointes.

Quetiapine (12.5–50 mg PO/NG twice daily) was associated with shorter delirium duration in a small RCT and is used in many centres for hyperactive or mixed delirium. Dexmedetomidine reduces delirium duration and promotes return to sleep in mechanically ventilated delirious patients (MENDS2 and DahLIA trials) and should be considered as the sedation agent when transitioning from other sedatives in delirious patients. Rivastigmine is not recommended (TRIAD trial showed harm). Melatonin for prevention of delirium lacks robust evidence in critically ill patients.

## Neuromuscular Blockade

### Indications in ARDS

Neuromuscular blocking agents (NMBAs) should not be used routinely in mechanically ventilated patients. Specific indications include: refractory hypoxaemia in moderate-severe ARDS (PaO2/FiO2 <150 mmHg despite optimised ventilation settings and prone positioning); patient-ventilator dyssynchrony causing haemodynamic instability or breath-stacking that cannot be managed with sedation adjustment; therapeutic hypothermia post-cardiac arrest (to eliminate shivering that impairs cooling); and refractory intracranial hypertension with dangerous surges in ICP.

The ACURASYS trial (2010) suggested benefit of early cisatracurium infusion (first 48 hours) in patients with PaO2/FiO2 <150. The larger ROSE trial (2019, n=1006) found no mortality benefit of routine 48-hour NMBA versus usual care in moderate-severe ARDS (standard care allowed deep sedation, confounding interpretation). Current guidance: consider cisatracurium infusion for up to 48 hours in patients with severe ARDS (PaO2/FiO2 <100–150 despite prone positioning and lung-protective ventilation) when sedation optimisation is insufficient. Prolonged NMBA use (>48 hours) is associated with ICU-acquired weakness and should be avoided.

### Monitoring and Safety

Train-of-four (TOF) stimulation is used to titrate neuromuscular blockade: deliver four supramaximal electrical stimuli to the ulnar nerve (at the wrist) and count twitches of the adductor pollicis. Target 1–2 twitches of 4 to avoid both under-dosing (inadequate block, patient awareness) and over-dosing (excessive blockade increasing weakness risk). Acceleromyography devices enable quantitative TOF measurement; qualitative visual or tactile assessment has high inter-observer variability.

All patients receiving NMBAs must have confirmed adequate sedation and analgesia before and throughout NMBA use — the agent eliminates all visible signs of discomfort, and inadequate analgosedation causes awareness and potentially psychological trauma. Confirm RASS target ≤–3 and consider BIS monitoring during NMBA. Cisatracurium (loading dose 0.2 mg/kg IV, infusion 0.03–0.06 mg/kg/hour) is the preferred NMBA in ICU because it undergoes Hofmann elimination (spontaneous degradation at physiological temperature and pH), making it safe in renal and hepatic failure without accumulation.
