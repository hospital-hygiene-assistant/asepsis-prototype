# Glycaemic Control in Critical Illness

> **SYNTHETIC TEST DOCUMENT — NOT CLINICAL GUIDANCE.** Written to exercise the
> ingest, indexing and retrieval pipeline. The recommendations below are
> plausible in shape but must never be used for patient care. Presented in the

## Abstract

Stress hyperglycaemia is present in roughly half of critically ill adults and is
associated with mortality independently of pre-existing diabetes. Trials of tight
control have repeatedly shown that the harm of hypoglycaemia offsets the benefit
of lower mean glucose. This review summarises current targets, the case for
individualising them by diabetes status, and the monitoring required to make any
target safe.

## Targets

### The General Target

Target a blood glucose of 8–10 mmol/L for most critically ill adults. Start
insulin when glucose exceeds 10 mmol/L on two consecutive readings.

Tight control to 4.5–6.0 mmol/L increases severe hypoglycaemia roughly six-fold
and increases mortality. It should not be used outside a trial protocol.

### Patients with Pre-existing Diabetes

Patients with a pre-admission HbA1c above 64 mmol/mol tolerate — and may
require — a higher range of 10–14 mmol/L. Rapid normalisation in a chronically
hyperglycaemic patient produces relative hypoglycaemia at glucose values that
would be unremarkable in anyone else.

Measure HbA1c on admission in every patient with hyperglycaemia. It distinguishes
stress hyperglycaemia from undiagnosed diabetes and sets the appropriate target
for the admission.

## Insulin Delivery

### Route

Use a continuous intravenous infusion while the patient is haemodynamically
unstable or receiving vasopressors. Subcutaneous absorption is unreliable in
shock, and apparent insulin resistance is frequently a perfusion problem rather
than a dosing one.

Transition to subcutaneous insulin only once the patient is eating and
vasopressors have been stopped. Overlap the infusion with the first subcutaneous
dose by 2 hours, since intravenous insulin has a half-life of minutes.

### Nutrition Interruptions

The commonest cause of hypoglycaemia in intensive care is an insulin infusion
continuing after enteral feed has been interrupted for a procedure. The insulin
protocol must be reviewed at every feed interruption, and this should be a
checklist item rather than a matter of memory.

## Monitoring

Measure hourly until glucose is within range for 4 consecutive hours, then every
2 hours. Return to hourly after any rate change, any change in nutrition, or the
initiation of corticosteroids.

Point-of-care capillary testing is unreliable in shock, oedema and anaemia. Use
arterial or venous blood gas glucose in any patient on vasopressors.

Continuous glucose monitoring shows promise in this population but is not yet
validated for insulin dosing during vasopressor therapy.

## Hypoglycaemia

Treat any glucose below 4.0 mmol/L immediately with 100 mL of 20% glucose
intravenously, then recheck after 15 minutes. Do not simply reduce the infusion
rate and wait.

Record every episode below 3.0 mmol/L as a patient safety incident. Severe
hypoglycaemia in critical illness carries an association with mortality
comparable to that of severe hyperglycaemia, and episodes cluster around
identifiable process failures rather than being distributed at random.

## Steroids and Other Confounders

Corticosteroids raise glucose predictably, with the peak effect 4–8 hours after
a morning dose of prednisolone. Anticipate the rise rather than reacting to it.

Continuous renal replacement therapy with glucose-containing replacement fluid
adds a variable and frequently unrecognised glucose load.

## Research Gaps

No trial has yet tested an HbA1c-stratified target prospectively against a fixed
target. Until one does, the recommendation to individualise by diabetes status
rests on observational data and physiological reasoning.
