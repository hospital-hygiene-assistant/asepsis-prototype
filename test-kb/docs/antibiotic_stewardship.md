# Antibiotic Stewardship

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a global public health emergency: without intervention, drug-resistant infections are projected to cause 10 million deaths annually by 2050, surpassing cancer. Antibiotic stewardship programmes (ASPs) are the primary institutional response — multidisciplinary systems that optimise antibiotic prescribing to improve patient outcomes, reduce Clostridioides difficile infection, slow the emergence of resistance, and lower drug costs. Every prescriber is a steward.

## Core Principles

The five core principles of antibiotic stewardship are: (1) prescribe antibiotics only when indicated (confirmed or suspected bacterial infection, not viral); (2) obtain appropriate cultures before starting antibiotics whenever possible; (3) select the narrowest-spectrum agent that covers the likely pathogens; (4) prescribe the correct dose and route (IV to oral switch as soon as the patient is haemodynamically stable and absorbing); and (5) use the shortest effective duration — longer courses do not improve outcomes and increase resistance and adverse effects.

## Respiratory Infections

### Community-Acquired Pneumonia

#### Severity Assessment (CURB-65)

Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) severity is stratified using the CURB-65 score, assigning one point each for: Confusion (new onset, AMTS ≤8); Urea >7 mmol/L (19 mg/dL); Respiratory rate ≥30/min; Blood pressure (systolic <90 mmHg or diastolic ≤60 mmHg); and age ≥65.

CURB-65 0–1: low severity — outpatient management appropriate; 30-day mortality 1.5%. CURB-65 2: moderate severity — hospital admission recommended; 30-day mortality 9.2%. CURB-65 3–5: high severity — consider ICU-level care; 30-day mortality 22–57%. The PSI (Pneumonia Severity Index) is more validated for identifying low-risk patients suitable for outpatient treatment, incorporating a broader set of variables, but is more complex to calculate at the bedside. SMART-COP assesses the need for intensive respiratory or vasopressor support and complements CURB-65 for identifying patients requiring ICU-level care.

#### Outpatient Treatment

For CURB-65 0–1 without contraindications, oral amoxicillin 500 mg three times daily for 5 days is first-line for non-severe CAP in patients without features suggesting atypical pathogens (no prominent extrapulmonary features, no outbreak setting, age <65, no comorbidity). Amoxicillin covers Streptococcus pneumoniae, the most common CAP pathogen.

If atypical pathogens are suspected (Mycoplasma, Legionella, Chlamydophila — younger patients, bilateral/patchy infiltrates, GI symptoms, prominent constitutional symptoms, failure to respond to beta-lactam): doxycycline 200 mg on day 1 then 100 mg daily for 5 days, or azithromycin 500 mg day 1 then 250 mg days 2–5. For penicillin-allergic patients: doxycycline or a respiratory fluoroquinolone (levofloxacin 500 mg daily for 5 days, moxifloxacin 400 mg daily for 5 days — reserve fluoroquinolones to minimise resistance). Point-of-care CRP <20 mg/L argues strongly against bacterial pneumonia; CRP >100 mg/L supports antibiotic use.

#### Inpatient Treatment

For moderate-severity CAP (CURB-65 2) requiring hospital admission, the preferred empiric regimen is co-amoxiclav 1.2 g IV three times daily PLUS a macrolide (clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily or azithromycin 500 mg daily IV/PO). This combination covers both typical (pneumococcal, Haemophilus) and atypical pathogens and is supported by observational evidence for mortality benefit over beta-lactam monotherapy.

Alternatives for penicillin allergy: levofloxacin 750 mg IV/PO daily covers both typical and atypical organisms and can be used as monotherapy. Ceftriaxone 2 g IV daily plus macrolide is an alternative in confirmed non-anaphylactic penicillin allergy. Duration: 5 days for moderate severity if clinical response is good (CRP falling, temperature settling, SpO2 normalising, HR <100 bpm). For severe CAP (CURB-65 3–5, ICU admission): piperacillin-tazobactam 4.5 g IV three times daily plus levofloxacin 750 mg IV daily, or meropenem 1 g IV three times daily if risk of resistant organisms (prior MDR colonisation, recent broad-spectrum antibiotics, structural lung disease). Add antifungal cover (anidulafungin) if immunocompromised. Duration: 7 days minimum; extend to 10–14 days for Legionella, Staphylococcal, or Gram-negative CAP.

### Avoiding Antibiotics in Viral Illness

Viral upper respiratory tract infections (common cold, acute pharyngitis without streptococcal features, acute bronchitis in otherwise healthy adults, and most acute sinusitis episodes) do not require antibiotic treatment. The number needed to treat to prevent one complication with antibiotics for uncomplicated URTI is >4,000 while the number needed to harm (one adverse event) is approximately 8. Centor/McIsaac criteria should guide antibiotic use in sore throat: score ≥3 warrants throat swab or rapid antigen test; positive streptococcal test or score ≥4 without testing: phenoxymethylpenicillin 500 mg twice daily for 5 days. Delayed prescribing (prescription given but patient instructed to fill only if not improving at 48 hours) reduces antibiotic use by approximately 25% without worsening outcomes.

## Urinary Tract Infections

### Asymptomatic Bacteriuria

Asymptomatic bacteriuria (ASB) — significant bacteriuria (≥10⁵ CFU/mL) in the absence of urinary symptoms — should NOT be treated with antibiotics in most clinical situations. Treatment does not reduce symptomatic UTI, complications, or mortality in non-pregnant adults, catheterised patients, elderly care home residents, or diabetic patients, and drives antimicrobial resistance and Clostridioides difficile infection.

Exceptions where ASB treatment is indicated: (1) pregnancy — ASB in pregnancy increases risk of pyelonephritis and preterm birth; treat with nitrofurantoin (avoid at term), cefalexin, or amoxicillin for 5–7 days guided by culture sensitivities; (2) prior to urological procedures expected to breach the urothelium (e.g., TURP, ureteroscopy) — a single pre-procedure dose of co-amoxiclav or cefalexin is recommended. Routine urine dipstick or culture in asymptomatic patients (including prior to elective procedures not breaching urothelium) is discouraged.

### Uncomplicated Lower UTI

Uncomplicated lower UTI (dysuria, frequency, urgency without systemic features in a non-pregnant, non-catheterised, immunocompetent woman without urological abnormality) is diagnosed clinically. Urine dipstick positive for nitrite (high specificity for Gram-negative bacteriuria) or leukocyte esterase supports the diagnosis; culture is not required for uncomplicated cases.

First-line treatment: nitrofurantoin 100 mg modified-release twice daily for 5 days (avoid if eGFR <45 mL/min/1.73 m²; contraindicated at eGFR <30; avoid at term pregnancy due to risk of haemolytic anaemia in neonate). Alternative: trimethoprim 200 mg twice daily for 7 days (check local resistance rates — trimethoprim resistance exceeds 30% in some areas; avoid if used in the preceding 3 months or if previous ESBL-producing organism). Fosfomycin 3 g single dose is a validated alternative with low resistance rates. Pivmecillinam 400 mg twice daily for 3–7 days (where available). Fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin, norfloxacin) should be avoided for uncomplicated lower UTI: no clinical advantage over first-line agents and disproportionate collateral damage on the microbiome and resistance selection.

### Complicated UTI and Pyelonephritis

Complicated UTI includes pyelonephritis, catheter-associated UTI, UTI in men, UTI with structural or functional urological abnormality, UTI in immunocompromised patients, and UTI with systemic features. A midstream urine culture must be obtained before initiating antibiotics. Blood cultures are required if systemic features are present (fever >38°C, rigors, haemodynamic compromise).

Empiric treatment for pyelonephritis: co-amoxiclav 625 mg three times daily orally (mild/moderate, well enough for oral therapy) or 1.2 g IV three times daily (severe/hospitalised); cefalexin 500 mg four times daily or ceftriaxone 1–2 g IV daily. Ciprofloxacin 500 mg twice daily for 7 days is an alternative where local resistance rates remain <10%. Gentamicin (5–7 mg/kg IV once daily) is the preferred agent for hospitalised patients when resistance or allergy limits beta-lactam options. Adjust to narrowest effective agent based on culture results at 48–72 hours. Duration: 7 days for uncomplicated pyelonephritis; 10–14 days if bacteraemia present; 5 days if ciprofloxacin or levofloxacin used and clinical response is good.

## Sepsis Antibiotic Management

Sepsis is a time-critical emergency — every hour of delay in antibiotic administration beyond the first hour is associated with a 7% increase in mortality. Blood cultures must be obtained before antibiotics but must not delay them by more than 45 minutes.

### Blood Cultures and Timing

At least two sets of blood cultures (aerobic and anaerobic bottles) should be obtained before antibiotic administration — one peripherally and one through any central venous catheter in place. The volume of blood per culture set is critical: inoculate at least 10 mL per bottle (20 mL total per set). A minimum of 2 sets (ideally 3 sets if endocarditis is suspected) maximises sensitivity (single set detects ~73% of bacteraemias; three sets ~97%). Do not delay antibiotics for more than 45 minutes while obtaining cultures. Urine culture, sputum culture (if productive cough), wound swabs, and imaging should be obtained simultaneously to identify the source.

### Empiric Regimens by Source

Antibiotic selection must cover the likely source of infection based on clinical findings, exposure history, and local resistance data. Unknown source or intra-abdominal sepsis: piperacillin-tazobactam 4.5 g IV every 6 hours (covers Gram-negatives including Pseudomonas, Gram-positives, and anaerobes). Urinary source (community-acquired): ceftriaxone 2 g IV daily (reserve piperacillin-tazobactam for patients with prior ESBL colonisation or failure of beta-lactam treatment). Biliary source: co-amoxiclav 1.2 g IV three times daily plus metronidazole 500 mg IV three times daily if biliary obstruction present. Pneumonia-source sepsis: as per severe CAP guidance above. Skin/soft tissue: flucloxacillin 2 g IV four times daily (Staphylococcal); co-amoxiclav for polymicrobial infections.

Add vancomycin (target trough 15–20 mg/L) or teicoplanin if MRSA is a realistic concern: healthcare-associated infection, known MRSA colonisation, IV drug use, prosthetic material. Meropenem 1–2 g IV three times daily is reserved for: patients with known carbapenem-sensitive ESBL-producing organism, prior culture with resistant Pseudomonas or Klebsiella, or clinical failure on piperacillin-tazobactam. Carbapenems should not be used empirically without a compelling documented resistance risk, as they are the last-resort agents for MDR organisms.

### De-escalation and Duration

De-escalation — switching from broad-spectrum empiric therapy to the narrowest effective agent based on culture results and clinical response — is a critical stewardship intervention that reduces adverse effects (Clostridioides difficile, renal toxicity, selection of resistant organisms), costs, and duration of hospital stay without increasing mortality or clinical failure rates.

Review all antibiotic prescriptions at 48–72 hours ("antibiotic time-out"): if cultures are negative and the patient has improved, consider stopping antibiotics or narrowing spectrum. If cultures are positive, de-escalate to the agent with the narrowest spectrum that covers the identified organism at the minimum effective dose. Duration for uncomplicated bloodstream infection: 7–10 days for Gram-negative bacteraemia with source control; 14 days minimum for S. aureus bacteraemia (mandatory echocardiogram to exclude endocarditis, which requires 4–6 weeks); 28–42 days for confirmed infective endocarditis. Procalcitonin-guided antibiotic cessation (stop when procalcitonin has fallen by ≥80% from peak or to <0.5 μg/L) is validated in CAP and ICU-acquired infections for reducing antibiotic duration without harm.

## Surgical Prophylaxis

### Timing and Single-Dose Principle

Surgical site infection (SSI) prophylaxis should be administered as a single IV dose within 60 minutes before skin incision (120 minutes for vancomycin and fluoroquinolones to allow adequate infusion time). The goal is to achieve therapeutic tissue concentrations at the time of incision and throughout the operation. A meta-analysis of >200 trials demonstrates equivalence of single preoperative dose versus multiple postoperative doses for clean and clean-contaminated procedures; postoperative continuation beyond 24 hours does not reduce SSI rates and increases Clostridioides difficile infection, adverse drug effects, and resistance.

Intraoperative redosing is required when the procedure duration exceeds two elimination half-lives of the antibiotic from the time of initial dose: cefuroxime every 4 hours; cefazolin every 4 hours; clindamycin every 6 hours. Redosing is also required when blood loss exceeds 1.5 litres or in major fluid resuscitation scenarios that dilute plasma drug concentrations.

### Agent Selection

The choice of prophylactic agent should be guided by the wound classification and likely contaminating organisms. Cefuroxime 1.5 g IV is the preferred prophylactic agent for most clean and clean-contaminated procedures: general surgery (cholecystectomy, hernia repair), orthopaedic surgery (hip and knee arthroplasty, open fracture fixation), vascular surgery (bypass grafting), and cardiothoracic surgery. It covers the most common SSI pathogens: Staphylococcus aureus, coagulase-negative staphylococci, and common Gram-negative organisms.

For colorectal and appendiceal surgery: co-amoxiclav 1.2 g IV (covers Gram-negatives and anaerobes); add metronidazole if perforated/faecal contamination. For urological procedures: gentamicin 3 mg/kg IV (covers Gram-negatives). For MRSA-colonised patients undergoing prosthetic implant surgery (joint replacement, cardiac valve surgery, vascular graft): teicoplanin 400–800 mg IV or vancomycin 15–20 mg/kg IV (up to 2 g) administered 60–120 minutes before incision, in addition to or replacing standard beta-lactam prophylaxis. Penicillin allergy: documented anaphylaxis to penicillin requires avoidance of cephalosporins — use clindamycin 600 mg IV or gentamicin 3 mg/kg IV depending on organism spectrum required. Non-anaphylactic penicillin allergy (rash): cefuroxime is safe (cross-reactivity <1%) and remains the preferred agent.
