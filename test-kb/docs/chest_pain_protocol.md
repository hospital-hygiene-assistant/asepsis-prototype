# Acute Chest Pain Protocol

## Initial Assessment

All patients presenting with acute chest pain must have a 12-lead ECG performed and interpreted within 10 minutes of arrival. Continuous cardiac monitoring, intravenous access, and oxygen saturation measurement are established simultaneously. Vital signs (heart rate, blood pressure bilaterally, respiratory rate, temperature, SpO2) are recorded immediately.

History should characterise onset (sudden vs. gradual), character (pressure, sharp, tearing, burning), severity (NRS 0–10), location and radiation (arm, jaw, back, epigastrium), duration, precipitating or relieving factors (exertion, rest, nitrates, position, food), and associated symptoms (dyspnoea, diaphoresis, nausea, palpitations, syncope, cough). Classical ischaemic chest pain: central or left-sided heaviness or pressure, radiation to left arm or jaw, onset with exertion, relieved by rest or nitrates. Atypical presentations are common in women, elderly patients, and people with diabetes.

Clinical risk stratification should be performed using a validated scoring tool. The HEART score (History, ECG, Age, Risk factors, Troponin) stratifies patients 0–10 and identifies low-risk patients safe for early discharge (HEART 0–3), intermediate-risk requiring observation (HEART 4–6), and high-risk requiring admission and early intervention (HEART ≥7). TIMI and GRACE scores are validated alternatives, with GRACE preferred for NSTEMI/UA once ACS is confirmed.

## ECG Interpretation

### STEMI Recognition

ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) is diagnosed when new ST elevation is present in two or more contiguous leads: ≥1 mm in limb leads or ≥2 mm in precordial leads V1–V4, or ≥1.5 mm in V5–V6 in women. New left bundle branch block (LBBB) with clinical presentation consistent with AMI should be treated as STEMI equivalent (apply Sgarbossa criteria: concordant ST elevation ≥1 mm = 5 points; concordant ST depression ≥1 mm in V1–V3 = 3 points; excessively discordant ST elevation ≥5 mm = 2 points; score ≥3 = STEMI equivalent).

Inferior STEMI (ST elevation in II, III, aVF) requires right-sided leads (V3R, V4R): ST elevation ≥0.5 mm in V4R indicates right ventricular MI, which contraindicates nitrates (may precipitate severe hypotension due to RV preload dependence). Posterior MI presents as ST depression in V1–V3 (mirror image): confirm with posterior leads V7–V9 showing ST elevation ≥0.5 mm. De Winter T-waves (ST depression at J-point in V1–V6 with tall, symmetrical T-waves and PR depression) are a proximal LAD occlusion equivalent requiring immediate cath lab activation.

### NSTEMI and UA Patterns

ST depression ≥0.5 mm in two or more contiguous leads indicates ischaemia and, combined with troponin rise, defines NSTEMI. Isolated T-wave inversion (≥1 mm deep, symmetric) in precordial leads suggests ischaemia particularly in V1–V4 (anterior wall). Wellens syndrome (biphasic T-waves in V2–V3 or deep symmetric T-wave inversions in V2–V3 without significant troponin rise) indicates critical proximal LAD stenosis with recent spontaneous reperfusion and requires urgent angiography even in pain-free periods.

New ST changes or T-wave inversions in the context of positive troponin define NSTEMI. Unstable angina (UA) is identical clinically but with negative high-sensitivity troponin. Serial ECGs every 15–30 minutes during ongoing symptoms, and at each troponin time-point. Continuous telemetry is mandatory during the acute presentation.

## Biomarker Strategy

### High-Sensitivity Troponin

High-sensitivity cardiac troponin (hs-cTn) I or T is the biomarker of choice. The ESC 0/1-hour algorithm uses assay-specific cut-offs at presentation and 1 hour: Rule-out requires hs-cTn below the assay's rule-out threshold at 0 hours AND an absolute change below the rule-out delta at 1 hour, in the absence of high-risk clinical features (ongoing chest pain, dynamic ECG changes, haemodynamic instability). Rule-in requires hs-cTn above the 99th percentile URL at presentation OR a significant absolute rise at 1 hour above the rule-in delta.

Patients in the "observe zone" (neither ruled in nor out at 1 hour) require a 3-hour sample. The 0/2-hour algorithm (ESC alternative) uses slightly different cut-offs. Absolute changes are more diagnostically useful than single values because troponin rises and falls with kinetics determined by timing of the index event. Early presenters (<3 hours from symptom onset) may have normal initial troponin: if clinical suspicion is high, repeat at 3 and 6 hours. Troponin is non-specific: elevation is seen in type 2 MI (supply-demand mismatch: sepsis, tachyarrhythmia, severe anaemia), myocarditis, pulmonary embolism, cardiac contusion, decompensated heart failure, stroke, and CKD.

### Other Biomarkers

D-dimer (ELISA) with pre-test probability assessment (Wells PE score or PERC rule) is used to exclude pulmonary embolism in low-intermediate risk patients. A negative D-dimer (age-adjusted threshold: age × 10 μg/L in patients >50) effectively excludes PE and avoids CT pulmonary angiography. NT-proBNP or BNP aids the diagnosis and severity assessment of heart failure in dyspnoeic patients. Bedside echocardiography is a complementary investigation during initial assessment to evaluate wall motion abnormalities, pericardial effusion, RV strain (PE), and LV function.

## STEMI Management

### Reperfusion

Primary percutaneous coronary intervention (pPCI) is the preferred reperfusion strategy when achievable within 120 minutes of first medical contact (FMC). The target door-to-balloon time is ≤90 minutes (≤60 minutes for anterior STEMI presenting within 2 hours of symptom onset). When pPCI is not achievable within this timeframe, fibrinolysis should be administered within 10 minutes of diagnosis: tenecteplase (weight-based bolus: 30 mg for <60 kg, 35 mg for 60–69 kg, 40 mg for 70–79 kg, 45 mg for 80–89 kg, 50 mg for ≥90 kg) or alteplase (15 mg IV bolus then 0.75 mg/kg over 30 min then 0.5 mg/kg over 60 min).

Absolute contraindications to fibrinolysis include any prior intracranial haemorrhage, ischaemic stroke within 3 months, structural cerebrovascular lesion, active internal bleeding, significant closed-head trauma within 3 months, or suspected aortic dissection. After fibrinolysis, patients should be transferred to a PCI-capable centre for pharmacoinvasive strategy (routine coronary angiography 3–24 hours post-fibrinolysis if successful, or rescue PCI within 60 minutes if fibrinolysis fails: persistent ST elevation >50% or haemodynamic instability at 60–90 minutes post-lytic).

### Antithrombotic Therapy

Dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) is initiated immediately upon diagnosis. Aspirin 300 mg loading dose (chewed or crushed for faster absorption), followed by 75–100 mg daily. A P2Y12 inhibitor is added: ticagrelor 180 mg loading dose (preferred over clopidogrel due to faster, more consistent platelet inhibition; PLATO trial), then 90 mg twice daily; or prasugrel 60 mg loading dose (superior to clopidogrel, TRITON-TIMI 38), then 10 mg daily (avoid in prior stroke/TIA, weight <60 kg, age ≥75). Clopidogrel 600 mg loading dose is used only when ticagrelor or prasugrel are contraindicated or unavailable.

Parenteral anticoagulation: unfractionated heparin (UFH) 70–100 U/kg bolus IV (maximum 10,000 U) prior to and during PCI; bivalirudin 0.75 mg/kg bolus then 1.75 mg/kg/hour during PCI (preferred in high bleeding risk, EUROMAX and HEAT-PPCI trials). Fondaparinux should not be used as the sole anticoagulant during PCI due to catheter thrombosis risk. DAPT should be maintained for 12 months post-PCI with drug-eluting stent, with consideration for extended therapy (up to 36 months) in high-ischaemic risk patients or reduction to 6 months in high-bleeding-risk patients (PRECISE-DAPT score ≥25).

## NSTEMI and UA Management

### Risk Stratification

All patients with NSTEMI require risk stratification to determine timing of invasive coronary angiography. The GRACE 2.0 score estimates in-hospital and 6-month mortality: high risk (>140) mandates early (<24 hour) invasive strategy; intermediate risk (109–140) warrants routine invasive strategy (<72 hours); low risk (<109) can be managed with selective invasive strategy based on non-invasive functional testing.

Immediate invasive strategy (<2 hours, equivalent to STEMI activation) is required for: refractory or recurrent chest pain, signs of heart failure or haemodynamic instability, life-threatening arrhythmias (VF or sustained VT), and dynamic ST changes or deep ST depression in the anterior leads (suggesting ongoing LAD territory ischaemia). Coronary CT angiography (CCTA) is an alternative to invasive angiography in low-intermediate risk patients without dynamic ECG changes or significant troponin rise, where exclusion of obstructive coronary artery disease is the primary goal.

### Medical Therapy

Antithrombotic therapy: aspirin 300 mg loading dose plus ticagrelor 180 mg (preferred for NSTEMI; same evidence base as for STEMI) or clopidogrel 600 mg if ticagrelor/prasugrel are contraindicated. Prasugrel is not recommended prior to coronary anatomy definition in NSTEMI. Anticoagulation: fondaparinux 2.5 mg SC daily (preferred if not proceeding to PCI within 24 hours due to lowest bleeding risk; OASIS-5 and OASIS-6 trials); enoxaparin 1 mg/kg SC twice daily; or UFH 60–70 U/kg IV bolus then infusion if early PCI planned.

High-intensity statin is initiated immediately (atorvastatin 80 mg). ACE inhibitor or ARB if LV ejection fraction <40%, hypertension, or diabetes. Beta-blocker for rate control, LV dysfunction, or ongoing ischaemia; avoid in acute decompensated heart failure or bradycardia. After PCI with drug-eluting stent: DAPT for 12 months (may be reduced to 6 months in high-bleeding-risk patients). Discharge medications: DAPT, statin, ACE inhibitor/ARB if indicated, beta-blocker, consider eplerenone if post-MI with EF ≤35%.
