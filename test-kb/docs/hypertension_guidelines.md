# Hypertension Management Guidelines

Hypertension affects over one billion adults worldwide and is a leading modifiable risk factor for stroke, myocardial infarction, heart failure, and chronic kidney disease. Sustained blood pressure ≥130/80 mmHg constitutes hypertension under current ACC/AHA criteria. Effective management requires accurate classification, targeted lifestyle intervention, and rational pharmacotherapy tailored to comorbidities.

## Classification and Diagnosis

Blood pressure is classified as normal (<120/80 mmHg), elevated (120–129/<80 mmHg), Stage 1 hypertension (130–139/80–89 mmHg), or Stage 2 hypertension (≥140/90 mmHg). Diagnosis requires two or more elevated readings on at least two separate visits using a validated sphygmomanometer. The patient should be seated, arm at heart level, after five minutes of rest, with no caffeine or exercise in the preceding 30 minutes.

Ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM) is the gold standard for diagnosing white-coat hypertension (elevated clinic BP with normal ambulatory readings) and masked hypertension (normal clinic BP with elevated ambulatory readings). ABPM daytime mean ≥135/85 mmHg or 24-hour mean ≥130/80 mmHg confirms hypertension.

Home blood pressure monitoring (HBPM) is an acceptable alternative. Patients should take duplicate readings morning and evening for seven days and average readings from days 2–7. A home average ≥135/85 mmHg is diagnostic.

Secondary causes should be excluded in patients with resistant hypertension, onset before age 30, severe hypertension at any age, or features pointing to a specific aetiology: primary aldosteronism, renal parenchymal disease, renovascular hypertension, obstructive sleep apnoea, phaeochromocytoma, Cushing syndrome, or thyroid dysfunction.

## Non-Pharmacological Management

Lifestyle modification reduces blood pressure substantially, delays the need for drug therapy, and enhances the effect of antihypertensives. All patients with hypertension or elevated blood pressure should receive structured lifestyle counselling at every visit.

### Dietary Approaches

#### Sodium Restriction

Sodium intake should be limited to less than 2,300 mg per day, with an optimal target of 1,500 mg/day in patients with Stage 1 or Stage 2 hypertension. A reduction from the average Western intake of 3,400 mg/day to 2,300 mg/day reduces systolic blood pressure by approximately 5–6 mmHg. The effect is greater in older individuals, Black patients, those with diabetes, and those with existing hypertension, where reductions of 8–10 mmHg have been observed.

Practical sodium reduction strategies include avoiding processed foods (which account for approximately 70% of dietary sodium), cooking without added salt, reading food labels to identify high-sodium products (>600 mg per serving), and choosing low-sodium alternatives. Potassium-enriched salt substitutes can provide additional blood pressure lowering, particularly in patients who are not at risk of hyperkalaemia.

#### DASH Diet

The Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) diet emphasises fruits (4–5 servings/day), vegetables (4–5 servings/day), low-fat dairy (2–3 servings/day), whole grains, nuts and legumes (4–5 servings/week), lean meats, and limits saturated fat, total fat, and sweets. Compared to a typical American diet, DASH reduces systolic blood pressure by 8–14 mmHg.

The combination of DASH diet and sodium restriction produces synergistic effects: the DASH-sodium trial demonstrated a 12 mmHg reduction in systolic BP in hypertensive participants eating the DASH diet with the lowest sodium level versus the control diet with the highest sodium level. Adherence is facilitated by meal planning, access to dietitian support, and family-wide adoption of dietary changes.

### Exercise and Weight Management

Aerobic exercise of moderate intensity (brisk walking, cycling, swimming) for at least 150 minutes per week, or 75 minutes per week of vigorous exercise, reduces systolic blood pressure by 5–8 mmHg. Dynamic resistance training (3 sessions/week, 8–10 exercises, 2–3 sets of 10–12 repetitions) provides additional benefit of approximately 2–4 mmHg. Isometric exercise (e.g., handgrip training) has shown surprisingly large reductions of 10 mmHg systolic in recent meta-analyses and can be integrated without equipment.

Weight reduction of 1 kg is associated with a 1 mmHg reduction in systolic BP. Achieving a body mass index of 18.5–24.9 kg/m² and a waist circumference below 88 cm (women) or 102 cm (men) is recommended. Caloric restriction with a protein-adequate diet, limiting alcohol to ≤1 standard drink/day for women and ≤2/day for men, and eliminating tobacco further reduce cardiovascular risk. Alcohol reduction from heavy intake to within recommended limits lowers systolic BP by approximately 3–4 mmHg.

## Pharmacological Therapy

### First-Line Drug Classes

Four drug classes are established as first-line antihypertensive therapy: thiazide-type diuretics, angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors, angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs), and dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers (CCBs). Thiazide diuretics: chlorthalidone (12.5–25 mg/day) is preferred over hydrochlorothiazide because of its longer half-life, more sustained 24-hour blood pressure reduction, and superior cardiovascular outcome data. Indapamide is an alternative with neutral metabolic profile.

ACE inhibitors (e.g., ramipril, perindopril, lisinopril) block conversion of angiotensin I to angiotensin II and reduce aldosterone secretion, lowering blood pressure and providing cardioprotective and nephroprotective effects. They are first-choice agents in patients with heart failure, LV systolic dysfunction, post-MI, diabetes with proteinuria, and CKD. ACE inhibitor-induced dry cough occurs in 10–20% of patients (higher incidence in Asian populations) and requires switching to an ARB.

ARBs (e.g., losartan, valsartan, irbesartan, candesartan) block the AT1 receptor directly and are pharmacologically equivalent to ACE inhibitors for most indications. They are better tolerated (no cough) and have comparable evidence for nephroprotection. ACE inhibitors and ARBs must never be combined (ONTARGET trial demonstrated increased risk of hyperkalaemia, hypotension, and acute kidney injury without additional cardiovascular benefit).

Dihydropyridine CCBs (e.g., amlodipine, felodipine) block L-type calcium channels in vascular smooth muscle, causing vasodilation. They are particularly effective in elderly and Black patients, in whom the renin-angiotensin system is relatively suppressed. They can be combined with ACE inhibitors or ARBs without restriction. Non-dihydropyridine CCBs (verapamil, diltiazem) are alternatives in patients with atrial fibrillation requiring rate control but should not be combined with beta-blockers.

Beta-blockers are no longer considered first-line for uncomplicated hypertension (inferior stroke prevention compared to other classes in meta-analyses) but remain first-line when there is a compelling indication: heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, post-MI, stable angina, or rate control in atrial fibrillation. Vasodilating beta-blockers (nebivolol, carvedilol) have a more favourable metabolic profile.

### Combination Strategies

Single-pill combinations (SPCs) improve adherence and blood pressure control compared to separate tablets of equivalent doses. For most patients with Stage 2 hypertension or Stage 1 hypertension more than 20/10 mmHg above target, initial two-drug therapy is recommended. The preferred combination is ACE inhibitor or ARB plus a CCB (demonstrated superiority in the ACCOMPLISH trial). The ACE inhibitor/ARB plus thiazide diuretic combination is equally valid for patients with oedema or who cannot tolerate CCBs.

Blood pressure target is <130/80 mmHg for most adults. A more relaxed target of <140/90 mmHg is acceptable in adults over 80 or those with frailty or significant comorbidity that limits tolerability. For adults aged 65–79 without frailty, the SPRINT trial supports targeting <120/120 mmHg systolic if tolerated, with reduced mortality and major cardiovascular events.

Titration should occur at intervals of 2–4 weeks, adding or uptitrating agents to achieve target. Electrolytes (potassium, sodium) and creatinine should be checked 2–4 weeks after starting or uptitrating RAAS inhibitors.

### Treatment-Resistant Hypertension

#### Evaluation and Causes

Resistant hypertension is defined as BP above target despite optimal doses of three antihypertensive agents of different classes including a diuretic, confirmed by ABPM or HBPM to exclude white-coat effect. Before escalating treatment, confirm true resistance: assess medication adherence (urine drug screening if in doubt), review interfering substances (NSAIDs, decongestants, oral contraceptives, liquorice, stimulants), ensure adequate diuresis, and check for secondary hypertension.

The most common cause of true resistant hypertension is primary aldosteronism, present in approximately 20% of cases. Screening: plasma aldosterone-to-renin ratio (ARR). If ARR is elevated, confirmatory testing (saline infusion test or fludrocortisone suppression test) and CT adrenal/adrenal vein sampling to differentiate unilateral from bilateral disease. Obstructive sleep apnoea should be evaluated with overnight oximetry or polysomnography. Other secondary causes: renal artery stenosis (renal Doppler, CT/MR angiography), phaeochromocytoma (24-hour urine catecholamines or plasma metanephrines), and Cushing syndrome (overnight dexamethasone suppression test).

#### Fourth-Line Agents

Spironolactone 25–50 mg/day is the preferred fourth-line agent regardless of whether primary aldosteronism is confirmed. The PATHWAY-2 trial demonstrated superiority of spironolactone over doxazosin and bisoprolol as fourth-line therapy, with mean additional systolic BP reduction of 8.7 mmHg. It is most effective in patients with elevated aldosterone-to-renin ratios. Monitor potassium and creatinine at 4–6 weeks; avoid if eGFR <30 or potassium >5.0 mmol/L.

Amiloride 10 mg/day is an alternative potassium-sparing diuretic with fewer endocrine side effects than spironolactone (no gynaecomastia or menstrual irregularity). Doxazosin (alpha-blocker) and bisoprolol (beta-blocker) are further options. Renal denervation (percutaneous catheter-based ablation of renal sympathetic nerves) is an emerging interventional option for patients with true resistant hypertension not controlled despite maximally tolerated pharmacotherapy; recent sham-controlled trials show BP reductions of 5–8 mmHg systolic.

## Special Populations

### Hypertension in Pregnancy

Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy are classified as chronic hypertension (pre-existing or diagnosed before 20 weeks), gestational hypertension (new onset ≥20 weeks without proteinuria), preeclampsia (gestational hypertension with proteinuria or end-organ dysfunction), and superimposed preeclampsia on chronic hypertension.

Safe antihypertensives in pregnancy include labetalol (first-line, 100–400 mg twice daily), nifedipine long-acting (20–60 mg/day), and methyldopa (250–500 mg 2–3 times daily). ACE inhibitors and ARBs are absolutely contraindicated (fetotoxic: renal dysgenesis, oligohydramnios, IUGR, fetal death). Atenolol is relatively contraindicated due to fetal growth restriction.

Treatment threshold is systolic ≥150 or diastolic ≥100 mmHg in chronic hypertension; acute severe hypertension (≥160/110 mmHg) requires immediate IV treatment with labetalol or hydralazine to prevent maternal stroke. Preeclampsia with severe features requires magnesium sulphate for seizure prophylaxis and consideration of delivery.

### Hypertension in CKD

Target blood pressure in CKD is <130/80 mmHg. RAAS inhibition (ACE inhibitor or ARB) is first-line therapy in patients with CKD and proteinuria (urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio ≥30 mg/g), as it reduces intraglomerular pressure and slows progression of proteinuric nephropathy (demonstrated in RENAAL and IDNT trials for diabetic nephropathy, and in REIN trial for non-diabetic CKD). RAAS inhibitors should not be combined.

A rise in serum creatinine of up to 30% within four weeks of starting an ACE inhibitor or ARB is acceptable (reflects reduced intraglomerular pressure and is associated with long-term renoprotection). A rise >30% or hyperkalaemia (>5.5 mmol/L) requires dose reduction or discontinuation and investigation for bilateral renal artery stenosis.

As GFR declines below 30 mL/min/1.73 m², thiazide diuretics lose efficacy (tubular secretion reduced) and should be replaced by loop diuretics (furosemide, torasemide). CCBs, beta-blockers, and centrally acting agents remain effective throughout CKD stages. In haemodialysis patients, antihypertensive doses should be reviewed as intradialytic clearance may alter drug levels.

### Elderly Patients

In patients aged 65–79, target BP of <130/80 mmHg is recommended when tolerated, based on SPRINT sub-group analysis. In patients ≥80, the HYVET trial demonstrated benefit of antihypertensive treatment targeting <150/80 mmHg with indapamide ± perindopril, reducing stroke by 30% and all-cause mortality by 21%.

Treatment in elderly patients should account for orthostatic hypotension (measure standing BP after 1 and 3 minutes), polypharmacy, cognitive impairment, and frailty. Start at lower doses and titrate slowly. CCBs and ARBs are generally well tolerated; alpha-blockers and centrally acting agents increase fall risk and should be avoided. Frailty assessment (e.g., Clinical Frailty Scale) should inform individualised targets.
