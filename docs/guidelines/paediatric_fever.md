# Fever in Children Under 5

> **SYNTHETIC TEST DOCUMENT — NOT CLINICAL GUIDANCE.** Written to exercise the
> ingest, indexing and retrieval pipeline. The recommendations below are
> plausible in shape but must never be used for patient care.

## Initial Assessment

Fever is defined as a temperature of 38.0°C or above measured at the axilla or
tympanic membrane. In children under 3 months a temperature of 38.0°C or above
is treated as a red flag on its own and warrants immediate senior review.

Record temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate, capillary refill time and
conscious level at first contact. Do not rely on the height of the fever alone
to judge severity — a child with a temperature of 40°C who is alert and playing
is at lower risk than a drowsy child at 38.2°C.

### Traffic Light Categories

Green (low risk) children have normal colour, respond normally to social cues,
have moist mucous membranes and no red or amber features.

Amber (intermediate risk) features include pallor reported by the parent,
reduced activity, nasal flaring, dry mucous membranes, poor feeding in infants,
reduced urine output, or a fever of 5 days or longer.

Red (high risk) features include mottled or blue skin, no response to social
cues, a weak or continuous cry, grunting, tachypnoea above 60 breaths per
minute, reduced skin turgor, or a non-blanching rash.

### Red Flags Requiring Immediate Escalation

A non-blanching rash, neck stiffness, status epilepticus, focal neurological
signs or a bulging fontanelle require immediate escalation and empirical
treatment before investigations are complete.

## Investigations

Children with any red feature require full blood count, C-reactive protein,
blood culture, blood gas and urine culture. Lumbar puncture is indicated in all
infants under 1 month with fever, in infants 1–3 months who appear unwell, and
in any child with reduced conscious level.

Chest radiography is not routine. Reserve it for children with oxygen
saturation below 92%, tachypnoea out of proportion to fever, or focal chest
signs on examination.

Urine testing is mandatory in every child under 3 years with fever and no
obvious source, because urinary tract infection is common and frequently
presents without localising symptoms at this age.

## Antimicrobial Treatment

### Empiric Therapy by Age

Infants under 1 month receive intravenous cefotaxime plus amoxicillin, the
amoxicillin covering Listeria monocytogenes, which is not reliably treated by
cephalosporins alone.

Infants of 1–3 months receive intravenous ceftriaxone, with amoxicillin added
if the child appears unwell or if Listeria cannot be excluded on clinical
grounds.

Children over 3 months with a high-risk presentation receive intravenous
ceftriaxone. Add vancomycin where meningitis is suspected and local pneumococcal
resistance exceeds 5%.

### Weight-Based Dosing

Ceftriaxone is dosed at 80 mg/kg once daily to a maximum of 4 g. Cefotaxime is
dosed at 50 mg/kg every 6 hours in the neonatal period. Amoxicillin for
suspected Listeria is 50 mg/kg every 8 hours.

Every paediatric dose must be calculated on an accurate current weight, not an
estimate from age, and checked by a second practitioner before administration.

### When Not to Give Antibiotics

The majority of febrile children under 5 have a self-limiting viral illness.
Children in the green category with an identified viral source do not need
antibiotics, and prescribing them drives resistance without shortening the
illness.

Do not prescribe antibiotics for otitis media with effusion, for uncomplicated
upper respiratory tract infection, or for fever alone in a well child.

## Antipyretics and Supportive Care

Paracetamol at 15 mg/kg every 4–6 hours or ibuprofen at 10 mg/kg every 6–8
hours may be used to relieve distress. Do not use them solely to reduce
temperature in a comfortable child, and do not alternate the two agents
routinely.

Encourage oral fluids. Do not use tepid sponging, and do not underdress or
overwrap a febrile child.

## Discharge and Safety Netting

Children in the green category may be managed at home. Give the parent verbal
and written advice describing the warning signs that require reassessment: a
non-blanching rash, reduced fluid intake, drowsiness, or fever persisting beyond
5 days.

Arrange review within 24 hours for any child discharged with amber features, and
document who will perform that review.
