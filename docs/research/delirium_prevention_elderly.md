# Delirium Prevention in Older Inpatients

> **SYNTHETIC TEST DOCUMENT — NOT CLINICAL GUIDANCE.** Written to exercise the
> ingest, indexing and retrieval pipeline. The recommendations below are
> plausible in shape but must never be used for patient care.

## Background

Delirium affects roughly 20% of older medical inpatients and up to 50% of those
following hip fracture surgery. It is independently associated with longer
admission, functional decline, institutionalisation and mortality, and around a
third of episodes are preventable.

## Recognition

### Screening

Screen every patient aged 65 or over within 24 hours of admission, and daily
thereafter on wards where the incidence exceeds 15%. The 4AT is the preferred
instrument: it requires no special training and takes under 2 minutes.

Hypoactive delirium is the more common presentation in the elderly and the more
frequently missed, because a quiet, withdrawn patient does not prompt review the
way an agitated one does.

### Distinguishing Delirium from Dementia

Delirium has an acute onset over hours to days and a fluctuating course, with
impaired attention as the cardinal feature. Dementia is insidious and stable
day to day. The two coexist frequently, and a documented baseline from a
relative or carer is the most reliable way to tell them apart.

## Non-Pharmacological Prevention

Multicomponent interventions reduce incidence by about a third and are the only
approach with consistent evidence of benefit.

### Orientation and Cognition

Ensure a visible clock and calendar, reintroduce staff by name at each contact,
and encourage family visiting outside protected mealtimes. Avoid moving patients
between wards: each additional ward move raises the risk measurably.

### Sleep and Environment

Cluster observations to protect a continuous sleep period. Dim lighting
overnight, offer earplugs, and stop routine overnight observations in stable
patients. Do not use hypnotics as a sleep strategy.

### Sensory, Mobility and Hydration

Ensure glasses and working hearing aids are available from the first day of
admission. Mobilise within 24 hours where safe. Review fluid balance daily, and
remove urinary catheters at the earliest opportunity.

## Medication Review

Anticholinergic burden is the most modifiable pharmacological risk. Review
oxybutynin, amitriptyline, promethazine and hyoscine at admission and stop them
where possible.

Benzodiazepines, opioids and Z-drugs all raise risk. Where opioid analgesia is
required, treat pain adequately — undertreated pain is itself a stronger
precipitant than the analgesia used to control it.

## Managing an Established Episode

### First Steps

Identify and treat the precipitant: infection, retention, constipation, pain,
hypoxia, electrolyte disturbance or drug withdrawal. Delirium is a symptom, and
sedating the patient without finding the cause treats the observer rather than
the patient.

Verbal de-escalation and the presence of a familiar person are first-line for
distress.

### When Medication Is Considered

Reserve antipsychotics for a patient who is a danger to themselves or others,
or whose distress is severe and unresponsive to non-pharmacological measures.
Use haloperidol 0.5 mg orally, reviewed after 2 hours, for no more than 5 days.

Avoid antipsychotics entirely in Parkinson's disease and dementia with Lewy
bodies, where they may precipitate severe extrapyramidal deterioration. Use
lorazepam instead in those patients, and in delirium tremens.

Every antipsychotic prescription must carry a stop date at the point of
prescribing. Continuation on discharge is a common and avoidable harm.

## Discharge and Follow-Up

Document the episode, its precipitant and its resolution in the discharge
summary. Persistent cognitive impairment at 3 months is common and warrants
formal cognitive assessment rather than being attributed to the admission.
